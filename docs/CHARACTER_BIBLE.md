# 1. Purpose

The Character Bible defines how people in Underhallow should feel, behave, develop, relate to one another, and contribute to the world.

It establishes the rules for creating:

* Major NPCs
* Minor NPCs
* Background residents
* Families and communities
* Romanceable characters
* Professionals and workers
* Outsiders and newcomers
* Mystery-connected characters
* Character relationships
* Character routines
* Character visual identities
* Character development

This document does **not** define individual named characters.

Individual character designs should be created later using this Bible as the governing standard.

---

# 2. Core Character Philosophy

Underhallow's characters should feel like **people who have their own lives, histories, and reasons for staying here rather than NPCs who exist only for the player.**

Characters are not primarily quest dispensers.

They are residents of a real-feeling community.

They have:

* Personal histories
* Relationships
* Opinions
* Responsibilities
* Fears
* Preferences
* Flaws
* Secrets
* Dreams
* Conflicts
* Memories
* Daily routines
* Changing circumstances

The player enters their world.

The world does not begin when the player arrives.

---

# 3. Character North Star

The desired player experience is:

> **“I care about these people, and I can't believe how much there is to each of them.”**

Characters should gradually move from being recognizable NPCs to people the player understands and emotionally remembers.

A successful character should be capable of producing reactions such as:

* “I like this person.”
* “I didn't expect them to be like that.”
* “I understand why they did that.”
* “I didn't know they had been through that.”
* “I wonder what they're hiding.”
* “I hope they're okay.”
* “I can't believe they changed.”
* “I want to see what happens to them.”

---

# 4. Character Tone

Underhallow's characters should combine:

* Warmth
* Humor
* Quirkiness
* Humanity
* Grounded behavior
* Emotional depth
* Mystery
* Occasional sadness
* Occasional seriousness
* Moral complexity

Characters should not all be cheerful.

Characters should not all be tragic.

Characters should not all be mysterious.

The community should contain ordinary people living ordinary lives alongside people carrying extraordinary histories.

---

# 5. Personality Philosophy

NPC personalities should be **strong but believable**.

Characters should have recognizable personalities without becoming caricatures.

A character may be:

* Extremely confident
* Quiet and observant
* Stubborn
* Warm
* Sarcastic
* Nervous
* Ambitious
* Curious
* Traditional
* Reckless
* Reserved
* Generous
* Judgmental
* Idealistic
* Practical
* Secretive

However, personality should not be reduced to a single trait.

A stubborn character can also be compassionate.

A funny character can also be grieving.

A kind character can make selfish decisions.

A respected character can be deeply insecure.

A suspicious character can be correct.

A seemingly ordinary character can possess extraordinary depth.

---

# 6. Character Flaws

NPCs should have meaningful flaws.

Some characters should be relatively ordinary and imperfect.

Others may possess serious personal or moral flaws.

Flaws may include:

* Pride
* Jealousy
* Fear
* Cowardice
* Stubbornness
* Resentment
* Dishonesty
* Avoidance
* Greed
* Guilt
* Controlling behavior
* Prejudice
* Recklessness
* Excessive ambition
* Emotional dependence
* Fear of change

Flaws should have consequences.

Characters should not be artificially flawed merely to make them interesting.

Their flaws should emerge naturally from who they are and what they have experienced.

---

# 7. NPC Disagreement

NPCs are allowed to disagree with the player.

They may:

* Challenge the player's opinions
* Reject requests
* Criticize decisions
* Disagree with interpretations
* Refuse to help
* Become angry
* Become disappointed
* Defend other characters
* Disagree about the island's history
* Change their opinion over time

Disagreement should feel like human interaction rather than a punishment mechanic.

The player should not automatically be right.

---

# 8. Population Structure

The main town should feel like a **medium-sized community**.

The intended meaningful population is approximately:

**30–50 memorable or individually identifiable NPCs.**

This provides enough social density to make the town feel alive while keeping important characters narratively manageable.

The wider island can contain additional residents and communities.

---

# 9. Character Population Layers

NPCs should exist in several levels of importance.

## 9.1 Major Characters

These are characters with substantial narrative, relationship, or gameplay importance.

They may have:

* Deep backstories
* Personal questlines
* Major relationships
* Strong routines
* Significant character development
* Secrets
* Mystery connections
* Romance potential
* Major consequences

---

## 9.2 Minor Characters

Minor NPCs are individually identifiable but have smaller narrative roles.

They may have:

* Small stories
* Local relationships
* Side quests
* Distinct professions
* Personal opinions
* Limited development

They should still feel like people rather than generic placeholders.

---

## 9.3 Background Residents

Background residents exist to make the world feel populated.

They may:

* Walk through town
* Work
* Shop
* Visit locations
* Socialize
* Attend events
* React to major events
* Have simple conversations
* Reference local life

Not every resident needs a complete character arc.

This distinction allows the world to feel populated without requiring every person to become a major narrative project.

---

# 10. Named Identity

Almost all meaningful residents should have:

* A name
* A recognizable appearance
* A profession or primary activity
* Basic personality traits
* At least one relationship or social connection

Depth should vary according to narrative importance.

The player should be able to distinguish between:

> “Someone who happens to live here”

and

> “Someone whose life I actually know.”

---

# 11. Character Archetypes

Underhallow should use familiar character archetypes as foundations.

Examples include:

* Shopkeeper
* Farmer
* Fisher
* Hunter
* Innkeeper
* Builder
* Carpenter
* Blacksmith
* Merchant
* Elder
* Scholar
* Explorer
* Craftsperson
* Sailor
* Healer
* Cook
* Artisan
* Caretaker
* Inventor

However, archetypes should frequently be **subverted or complicated**.

The friendly shopkeeper may have an unexpectedly difficult past.

The mysterious old person may simply be eccentric.

The hardworking farmer may secretly hate farming.

The respected scholar may be wrong about something important.

The intimidating hunter may be gentle with animals.

The cheerful innkeeper may know much more than they admit.

Archetypes are starting points, not character definitions.

---

# 12. Character Complexity

Some characters should initially appear straightforward before revealing additional dimensions.

This is an important storytelling technique.

However, complexity should not mean that every character secretly possesses a dramatic twist.

Some people should simply be who they appear to be.

The contrast between ordinary people and complicated people makes both feel more believable.

---

# 13. Backstories

Major characters should have **deep personal histories**.

A major character's backstory may include:

* Childhood
* Family
* Education
* Relationships
* Career
* Failures
* Successes
* Regrets
* Loss
* Personal ambitions
* Secrets
* Previous relationships
* Decisions that shaped their present life

Backstory should explain behavior rather than exist merely as trivia.

---

# 14. Personal Secrets

Major NPCs may possess secrets unrelated to Underhallow's central mystery.

These may involve:

* Family problems
* Past relationships
* Failed ambitions
* Personal mistakes
* Hidden fears
* Financial difficulties
* Old conflicts
* Private regrets
* Personal achievements
* Things they are ashamed of
* Things they simply prefer not to discuss

Not every secret needs to be discovered.

Some aspects of another person's life should remain private.

---

# 15. Undiscovered Truths

A small number of NPCs should possess secrets the player can never uncover.

This is intentional.

The world should not behave as though every mystery exists specifically to be solved by the protagonist.

Some people should remain partially unknowable.

This reinforces the principle that:

> **The player is part of Underhallow, not the center of every life within it.**

---

# 16. Sadness and Emotional Depth

Some NPC stories should be genuinely sad.

However, sadness should coexist with:

* Humor
* Friendship
* Love
* Hope
* Everyday life
* Recovery
* Small joys

Underhallow should not become relentlessly tragic.

Emotional contrast is important.

A character may have experienced something terrible and still spend their afternoon arguing about vegetables.

That contrast contributes to the game's emotional identity.

---

# 17. Relationships

Relationships should provide a combination of:

* Friendship
* Emotional stories
* Social progression
* Practical benefits
* Information
* Access to personal stories
* Access to secrets
* New perspectives
* Character development

Relationships should not function solely as reward meters.

The player's relationship with an NPC should feel like a relationship with a person.

---

# 18. NPC-to-NPC Relationships

Meaningful NPCs should have relationships with one another.

These may include:

* Friendships
* Family relationships
* Rivalries
* Business partnerships
* Romantic relationships
* Former relationships
* Mentorships
* Resentments
* Dependencies
* Community bonds

NPCs should sometimes discuss people who are not present.

This helps establish the sense that relationships exist independently of the player.

---

# 19. Social Change

NPC relationships should be capable of changing.

Characters may:

* Become closer
* Drift apart
* Argue
* Reconcile
* Start relationships
* End relationships
* Forgive one another
* Become rivals
* Change their opinions
* Form new friendships

Social change should be meaningful rather than constant.

The town should feel alive without becoming a perpetual soap opera.

---

# 20. Independent NPC Lives

NPCs should have lives that continue when the player is not interacting with them.

Characters may:

* Work
* Eat
* Rest
* Visit friends
* Visit family
* Shop
* Attend events
* Travel
* Fish
* Farm
* Hunt
* Build
* Study
* Socialize
* Pursue hobbies

The player's presence should influence the community, but not completely control it.

---

# 21. Memory

NPCs should remember meaningful player actions.

Memory should prioritize:

* Major choices
* Important conversations
* Relationship milestones
* Completed personal quests
* Significant favors
* Betrayals
* Important discoveries
* Major local events

The system should not attempt to remember every insignificant interaction.

The goal is believable memory, not exhaustive surveillance.

---

# 22. Romance

Romance should exist as an **optional system**.

Romanceable characters must remain complete characters independent of romance.

Their identity should not become:

> “The NPC you can marry.”

Instead, romance should be one possible dimension of an already-developed relationship.

Romanceable characters should retain:

* Careers
* Friendships
* Personal goals
* Family
* Opinions
* Conflicts
* Individual storylines
* Independent routines

---

# 23. Marriage

Marriage should exist as a later-stage relationship outcome.

It should represent a meaningful continuation of a relationship rather than simply another progression tier.

Marriage should not erase the character's independent identity.

A married NPC should continue to feel like the same person with their own life.

---

# 24. Mystery Knowledge

Knowledge of Underhallow's central mystery should be fragmented.

Different people should know different pieces.

Knowledge may exist among:

* Families
* Elders
* Farmers
* Fishermen
* Hunters
* Scholars
* Religious or traditional communities
* Craftspeople
* Regional communities
* Individuals who personally experienced unusual events

No single NPC should possess the entire truth.

---

# 25. Lies and False Beliefs

Some NPCs should knowingly lie about Underhallow's history.

Their motives may differ.

A character may lie because they:

* Want to protect someone
* Fear what the truth could cause
* Were taught to remain silent
* Feel guilty
* Want to preserve the community
* Want to protect themselves
* Believe the player should not know
* Have their own interpretation of what happened

Other characters should genuinely believe things that are false.

This distinction is important.

**A lie and a mistaken belief are not the same thing.**

---

# 26. Conflicting Histories

Different NPCs may provide contradictory accounts of the island's past.

Contradictions should not always mean someone is lying.

They may result from:

* Incomplete knowledge
* Family traditions
* Lost records
* Generational distortion
* Personal interpretation
* Deliberate secrecy
* Political or social bias
* Genuine disagreement

The player should gradually assemble a more complete picture through evidence.

---

# 27. Trusted NPCs and Betrayal

The player should eventually encounter situations where someone they trust has hidden something important.

These moments should carry genuine emotional weight.

However, secrecy does not automatically equal betrayal.

The player should sometimes discover:

> “They lied to me.”

and other times:

> “They were protecting someone.”

or:

> “They genuinely believed they were doing the right thing.”

This supports Underhallow's morally complex narrative.

---

# 28. Character Mortality

Important NPCs may die **rarely and only when narratively justified**.

NPC mortality should never become a routine source of drama.

Death should matter because the character mattered.

When death occurs, the world should respond.

Relationships, routines, locations, dialogue, and community dynamics may change.

---

# 29. NPC Departure

NPCs can leave Underhallow.

Some departures may be:

* Temporary
* Permanent
* Voluntary
* Forced by circumstances
* Caused by personal conflict
* Caused by opportunity
* Influenced by the player's actions

In some cases, the player may influence whether a character stays or leaves.

This reinforces the idea that people have lives beyond the player's immediate control.

---

# 30. Permanent Character Change

Characters can permanently change because of player actions.

Possible changes include:

* Relationships
* Beliefs
* Careers
* Living circumstances
* Social standing
* Personal confidence
* Attitudes toward the player
* Attitudes toward the mystery
* Relationships with other NPCs

Not every choice needs a permanent consequence.

But meaningful choices should sometimes matter long after the original interaction.

---

# 31. Multiple Roles and Interests

NPCs should not be reduced to professions.

A person can be:

* A farmer and amateur historian
* A fisherman and musician
* A shopkeeper and gardener
* A hunter and storyteller
* A builder and community organizer
* A scholar and poor communicator
* An innkeeper and former sailor

Characters should have interests beyond their jobs.

---

# 32. Changing Circumstances

NPCs may change professions or life circumstances when narratively appropriate.

Examples include:

* Retirement
* Career changes
* Business expansion
* Business failure
* Marriage
* Separation
* Relocation
* New responsibilities
* Injury
* New ambitions
* Discovery of a new interest

Change should feel like the result of life, not arbitrary simulation.

---

# 33. Outsiders

Underhallow has had newcomers before the player.

Some residents may themselves be:

* Former travelers
* Immigrants
* Traders
* Researchers
* Refugees
* Adventurers
* People seeking a quieter life
* People running from their past

This prevents the player from feeling like the only outsider the island has ever seen.

It also provides additional perspectives on Underhallow.

---

# 34. The Player's Arrival

The town is welcoming.

However, acceptance should not mean instant intimacy.

The player should experience:

> **Warmth first. Trust second. Belonging later.**

Residents are generally friendly, but relationships develop through time and interaction.

The player should not need to “earn the right” to exist in town.

They simply need time to become part of it.

---

# 35. The Strange Knowledge of the Player

The player should discover that people know about their arrival unusually quickly.

Residents may already know:

* Who inherited the property
* Where the player came from
* That the player has arrived
* Basic details about their connection to the deceased grandparent

This should initially feel like ordinary small-town gossip.

Over time, it should become increasingly strange.

The player may eventually wonder:

> **“How did everyone know?”**

The explanation should connect naturally to the larger mystery without immediately revealing it.

---

# 36. Belonging

The player's feeling of belonging should emerge from multiple dimensions.

These include:

* Building and improving the house
* Developing friendships
* Helping residents
* Contributing to the town
* Participating in traditions
* Attending events
* Developing relationships
* Becoming familiar with local places
* Helping people through difficult moments
* Creating a life on the personal island

Belonging should feel earned emotionally rather than unlocked numerically.

---

# 37. Character Visual Identity

Major NPCs should have **very strong visual identities**.

Players should ideally recognize important characters from:

* Silhouette
* Hairstyle
* Clothing
* Accessories
* Color relationships
* Body language
* Occupation
* Distinctive props

Visual design should support memory.

A player should be able to recognize an NPC before reading their name.

---

# 38. Clothing

Clothing should reflect both:

**Occupation + Personality**

A fisher should look like someone who can actually work around water.

A blacksmith should have clothing appropriate for the forge.

But two people with the same profession should not necessarily dress alike.

Personal taste, age, wealth, personality, history, and lifestyle should influence appearance.

---

# 39. Age and Visual Change

NPC appearances may change over time when narratively appropriate.

Changes may include:

* Hair
* Clothing
* Accessories
* Posture
* Physical condition
* Facial details
* Signs of aging
* Lifestyle changes

Changes should be subtle and meaningful rather than purely cosmetic.

---

# 40. Daily Routines

Nearly every meaningful NPC should have a recognizable routine.

Routines should communicate personality without dialogue.

A character might:

* Visit the harbor every morning
* Eat at the same table
* Walk through town at sunset
* Work late
* Visit a particular friend
* Sit somewhere quiet
* Practice a hobby
* Check on their farm
* Visit a family member

Important NPCs should have richer routines.

Background residents can use simpler routines.

---

# 41. Character Expression

Character animation should communicate personality.

Important characters should receive richer:

* Idle animations
* Gestures
* Facial expressions
* Reactions
* Emotional animations
* Story-specific animations

Ordinary NPCs should have enough animation to communicate:

* Walking
* Working
* Sitting
* Eating
* Farming
* Talking
* Basic reactions

Characters should communicate through body language as well as dialogue.

---

# 42. Character Diversity

Character diversity should emerge naturally from the population.

Residents should differ in:

* Age
* Personality
* Occupation
* Background
* Appearance
* Wealth
* Family situation
* Beliefs
* Goals
* Experience
* Relationship to Underhallow

Diversity should not feel like characters were assembled from a checklist.

---

# 43. Humor

Characters should frequently be capable of humor.

Humor may emerge from:

* Personality
* Situations
* Misunderstandings
* Relationships
* Local traditions
* Rivalries
* Everyday life
* Dry observations

Characters should not become walking jokes.

The overall tone remains:

**cozy + mysterious + adventurous + wholesome.**

---

# 44. Seriousness

Characters must also be capable of genuine seriousness.

Underhallow may explore:

* Loss
* Regret
* Guilt
* Fear
* Betrayal
* Historical injustice
* Difficult choices
* Broken relationships
* Mortality
* Consequences

These moments should contrast against the game's cozy surface rather than destroy it.

---

# 45. Character and Mystery

The central mystery should not consume every character.

Some characters should be deeply connected to it.

Some should know fragments.

Some should have been affected by it without understanding it.

Some should know nothing meaningful about it.

Some should actively avoid it.

Some should dismiss it entirely.

This creates a believable distribution of knowledge.

---

# 46. Character Recontextualization

Major revelations should sometimes change how the player understands earlier character interactions.

A seemingly harmless statement may later become significant.

An unusual behavior may eventually have an explanation.

A family tradition may reveal historical importance.

A character's fear may make sense only after the player learns what happened.

This should be used carefully.

Not every line of dialogue needs a secret meaning.

---

# 47. Characters as Historical Memory

People are one of Underhallow's primary forms of historical evidence.

History survives through:

* Family stories
* Traditions
* Rumors
* Personal memories
* Old grudges
* Forgotten relationships
* Local sayings
* Songs
* Objects
* Stories told by elders

But human memory is imperfect.

Therefore:

> **Characters preserve history, but they do not necessarily preserve the truth.**

---

# 48. Character Agency

NPCs should be capable of wanting things that have nothing to do with the player.

They may pursue:

* Love
* Wealth
* Stability
* Recognition
* Family
* Knowledge
* Adventure
* Peace
* Revenge
* Forgiveness
* Security
* Freedom
* Community

Their goals should occasionally conflict with one another.

---

# 49. Character Design Rules

Every major NPC should answer:

1. Who are they?
2. What do they want?
3. What do they fear?
4. What do they believe?
5. What do they value?
6. What flaw makes their life harder?
7. What history shaped them?
8. Who matters to them?
9. What do they hide?
10. What would make them change?
11. What do they think about Underhallow?
12. What do they think about the player?
13. What do they do when nobody is watching?

---

# 50. Anti-Patterns

Underhallow should avoid:

### Quest Machines

NPCs should not exist primarily to give the player objectives.

### One-Trait Characters

“Funny guy,” “mean woman,” or “wise old man” is not enough.

### Universal Niceness

A cozy world does not require everyone to be agreeable.

### Universal Mystery

Not everyone needs a secret.

### Forced Drama

Characters should not constantly argue simply to make the town appear alive.

### Player-Centric Reality

NPCs should not stop having lives whenever the player leaves.

### Romance Dependency

Romanceable characters must remain interesting without romance.

### Lore Dumping

Characters should not explain the island's history simply because the player asks.

### Fake Complexity

A character should not have a secret merely because the story needs a twist.

---

# 51. Character Development Philosophy

Character development should answer:

> **“What happens to this person because they lived through these events?”**

rather than:

> **“What new information can we reveal about them?”**

Information is useful.

Change is more important.

A strong character arc should alter how the player understands the character and how the character understands themselves.

---

# 52. Relationship Philosophy

Relationships should develop through repeated interactions.

The player should gradually learn:

* What a person likes
* What they dislike
* What they care about
* Who they trust
* What makes them uncomfortable
* What they fear
* What they remember
* What they are hiding
* What they hope for

Relationships should feel like accumulation rather than progression through arbitrary levels.

---

# 53. Character-Driven Worldbuilding

Characters should help communicate the identity of different regions.

Different communities may have:

* Distinct traditions
* Different accents or speech patterns where appropriate
* Different occupations
* Different relationships with nature
* Different beliefs
* Different interpretations of Underhallow's history
* Different attitudes toward outsiders

Regional identity should emerge from people as much as geography.

---

# 54. The Player Is Not the Chosen One

The player should not automatically possess special social authority.

They become important because they:

* Build relationships
* Participate in the community
* Explore
* Make decisions
* Discover things
* Help people
* Become part of Underhallow

Their significance grows through experience.

---

# 55. Emotional Progression

Character relationships should support the broader emotional journey of the game:

**Arrival → Familiarity → Friendship → Belonging → Curiosity → Suspicion → Revelation → Understanding → Uncertainty → Acceptance**

Not every character follows the entire sequence.

Some remain simple friends.

Some become deeply important.

Some drift away.

Some become connected to the mystery.

Some remain ordinary people the player simply enjoys knowing.

---

# 56. Character Quality Standard

A major NPC should ideally satisfy three levels:

### Surface

The player can immediately recognize who they are.

### Depth

The player discovers there is more to them than initially apparent.

### Humanity

The player eventually understands why they behave the way they do, even when they disagree with them.

The strongest characters may go further:

### Recontextualization

Later events cause the player to reinterpret earlier assumptions about them.

---

# 57. Character Bible Core Principles

1. Characters are people, not quest machines.
2. NPCs have lives independent of the player.
3. Cozy does not mean shallow.
4. Humor and sadness should coexist.
5. Characters may disagree with the player.
6. Characters should have meaningful flaws.
7. Not every character needs a dramatic secret.
8. Some mysteries should remain personal.
9. Relationships should exist between NPCs.
10. NPCs should change over time.
11. The player should earn belonging naturally.
12. Mystery knowledge should be fragmented.
13. People preserve history imperfectly.
14. Romance should not define a character.
15. Major NPCs should have strong visual identities.
16. Routines should communicate personality.
17. Some NPCs can leave or die when narratively justified.
18. Player choices can create permanent character consequences.
19. The player is not the center of every NPC's life.
20. Characters should feel like people who were already living in Underhallow.

---

# 58. Character Bible North Star

> **Underhallow's characters should feel like people you happened to meet in a strange little town — people with lives, histories, relationships, flaws, secrets, hopes, and reasons to stay.**

The player arrives as an outsider.

Over time, some of these people become friends.

Some become family.

Some become mysteries.

Some become strangers again.

And some become part of the reason Underhallow feels like home.

---

# 59. Final Character Philosophy

**Characters are not content placed inside the world.**

**Characters are part of the world.**

Underhallow should create the feeling that if the player disappeared for a week, life would continue.

People would still work.

Friends would still meet.

Arguments would still happen.

Businesses would still open.

Families would still change.

Someone might fall in love.

Someone might leave.

Someone might remember something.

Someone might continue carrying a secret.

The player is not the reason Underhallow exists.

The player is someone who eventually becomes part of it.

That distinction is fundamental to the identity of the game.

---

## Final Character Statement

> **Underhallow's characters should feel like people who have their own lives, histories, and reasons for staying here rather than NPCs who exist only for the player.**
