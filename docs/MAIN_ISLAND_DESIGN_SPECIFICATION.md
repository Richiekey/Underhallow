# 1. Purpose

The Main Island is the central shared geography of Underhallow.

It is the place where the player encounters the wider world beyond their personal home: the town, its people, farms, forests, waterways, wilderness, ruins, wildlife, hidden places, regional communities, and the deeper mystery beneath the island.

The Main Island must not feel like a collection of gameplay zones connected by loading screens.

It must feel like **one coherent place**.

The player should gradually develop a mental map of it.

They should recognize landmarks, remember paths, know where certain people live, understand how rivers connect regions, remember where they found a strange ruin, and eventually feel that they know the island almost as well as a real place they have lived in for years.

The central design principle is:

> **The main island of Underhallow should feel like a place you could spend years getting to know rather than a map you are expected to complete.**

The island exists to support the game's broader philosophy:

> **Cozy surface. Mystical middle. Deep mystery underneath.**

---

# 2. Design Goals

The Main Island must achieve the following:

1. Feel geographically coherent.
2. Feel medium-sized rather than artificially enormous.
3. Provide meaningful exploration without becoming exhausting.
4. Support farming, hunting, fishing, gathering, crafting, trading, social interaction, and quests.
5. Provide a strong sense of place.
6. Contain substantial wilderness and breathing room.
7. Make geography meaningful to navigation.
8. Give every major region a distinct identity.
9. Support environmental storytelling.
10. Allow secrets to exist without making every location suspicious.
11. Gradually reveal the island's deeper history.
12. Make the island feel older than the player's arrival.
13. Make the island's isolation believable.
14. Allow some mysteries to remain unresolved.
15. Ensure the Main Island complements rather than duplicates the Personal Island.
16. Make returning to familiar places feel different as the player's understanding changes.

# 3. Role of the Main Island

The Main Island is the world of:

* Community
* Discovery
* Exploration
* History
* Relationships
* Commerce
* Adventure
* Wilderness
* Mystery
* Shared geography
* Regional identity
* Story progression

The Personal Island is fundamentally about:

* Home
* Ownership
* Stability
* Farming
* Building
* Personal expression
* Long-term progression

The two spaces must therefore have deliberately different emotional purposes.

### Main Island

> **“What is out there?”**

### Personal Island

> **“This is mine.”**

The Main Island should pull the player outward.

The Personal Island should pull the player home.

# 4. Geographic Philosophy

The Main Island is one large irregular island rather than a collection of disconnected biome zones.

Its geography should emerge naturally.

The island contains:

* Bays
* Peninsulas
* Rivers
* Lakes and ponds
* Rolling hills
* Highlands
* Farmland
* Meadows
* Woodland
* Ancient forest
* Wetlands
* Beaches
* Rocky coastline
* Cliffs
* Coves
* Caves
* Underground systems
* Ruins
* Settlements
* Wilderness

These features must interact geographically.

For example:

* Rivers should originate somewhere plausible.
* Lowlands should naturally collect water.
* Farms should generally appear where land is productive.
* Settlements should have reasons to exist.
* Bridges should cross meaningful waterways.
* Roads should follow terrain rather than ignoring it.
* Forests should occupy appropriate terrain.
* Cliffs should create natural boundaries.
* Wetlands should occur around suitable low-lying water systems.
* Ancient structures should appear where historical civilizations would plausibly have built.

The world should never feel as though individual biomes were placed independently for gameplay convenience.

---

# 5. Island Silhouette

The island should have an irregular, immediately recognizable silhouette.

Its coastline should contain several large geographic features:

* Major bays
* Peninsulas
* Rocky coves
* Beaches
* Marshy coastline
* Cliffs
* Harbor areas
* Smaller islands or offshore formations where appropriate

The overall shape should be distinctive enough that the player can recognize Underhallow from a world map or illustrated map.

The silhouette should become part of the game's visual identity.

---

# 6. Accessibility Philosophy

The island should reveal itself gradually.

During the early game, approximately half of the meaningful island geography should be comfortably accessible.

This is not a hard mathematical requirement.

The intent is:

> **The player should have freedom immediately without understanding everything immediately.**

Early exploration should include:

* Town
* Surrounding countryside
* Nearby farms
* Meadows
* Woodland
* Accessible coastline
* Initial fishing areas
* Early hunting grounds
* Some caves
* Some ruins
* Several secrets

As the player progresses, additional regions become accessible through combinations of:

* Natural geography
* Tools
* Upgrades
* Story progression
* NPC relationships
* Player knowledge
* Environmental understanding

The island should therefore feel progressively revealed rather than progressively unlocked through arbitrary barriers.

---

# 7. Long-Term Accessibility

Most of the Main Island should eventually become accessible.

However, some places may remain:

* Difficult to reach
* Dangerous
* Hidden
* Temporarily inaccessible
* Accessible only under certain conditions
* Geographically isolated
* Mysterious
* Permanently unreachable

This is intentional.

Underhallow should preserve the idea that the world is larger than the player's ability to completely understand or possess it.

The game must never imply:

> “Everything exists for the player to conquer.”

---

# 8. Major Regional Structure

The island should not be constrained to an arbitrary number of regions.

Geography should determine the final regional structure.

The expected result is approximately **7–9 substantial major regions**, with smaller subregions nested within them.

Major regions should be distinct in:

* Terrain
* Vegetation
* Color
* Lighting
* Wildlife
* Resources
* Traversal
* Activities
* Architecture
* Settlement patterns
* NPC culture
* History
* Atmosphere
* Secrets

Regions should not simply be the same gameplay space recolored.

---

# 9. Core Regional Framework

The Main Island should naturally support the following environmental identities.

## 9.1 Town and Countryside

The central civilized region.

Contains:

* Main town
* Town Square
* Harbor
* River
* Farms
* Meadows
* Roads
* Bridges
* Workshops
* Homes
* Markets
* Nearby natural areas

This is the player's initial anchor.

---

## 9.2 Farmland and Meadows

Open countryside surrounding the town and nearby settlements.

Characteristics:

* Agricultural fields
* Pastures
* Wildflowers
* Fences
* Farmhouses
* Barns
* Dirt paths
* Small streams
* Scattered trees
* Grazing animals
* Gathering resources

This region provides a gentle transition between civilization and wilderness.

---

## 9.3 Woodland

The intermediate wilderness.

The woodland should feel more secluded than the countryside but not immediately threatening.

Characteristics may include:

* Dense trees
* Narrow trails
* Small clearings
* Wildlife
* Streams
* Fallen trees
* Old paths
* Small ruins
* Hidden shelters
* Gathering areas
* Hunting grounds

It acts as a major transitional layer.

---

## 9.4 Ancient Forest

One of the island's deepest natural environments.

The ancient forest should feel older than the surrounding woodland.

It should contain:

* Enormous old trees
* Unusual clearings
* Ancient paths
* Forgotten structures
* Strange natural formations
* Rare wildlife
* Hidden locations
* Deep environmental storytelling

The forest should not immediately announce itself as supernatural.

It should first feel beautiful.

Then old.

Then strange.

The relationship between the ancient forest and the island's underground world should become increasingly important.

---

## 9.5 Highlands

A region of rolling hills and higher terrain.

The highlands should provide:

* Long sightlines
* Elevated viewpoints
* Natural barriers
* Hillside paths
* Small valleys
* Springs
* Rocky terrain
* Ruins
* Rare resources
* Distinctive wildlife

The island should not become a giant mountain-climbing environment.

Elevation exists primarily to shape geography, exploration, visibility, and discovery.

---

## 9.6 Coast

The coastline is one of the defining identities of Underhallow.

It should contain a mixture of:

* Beaches
* Cliffs
* Rocky shores
* Coves
* Harbors
* Marshy coastline
* Fishing locations
* Tide pools
* Offshore formations
* Hidden coastal paths

Water constantly reinforces that the player lives on an isolated island.

The coastline should also become one of the strongest sources of visual atmosphere.

---

## 9.7 Wetlands

Wetlands should occupy appropriate low-lying areas around rivers, lakes, and coastal systems.

They should feel distinct from forests and beaches.

Potential features include:

* Marshes
* Reeds
* Shallow water
* Boardwalks
* Muddy trails
* Small islands
* Water birds
* Rare plants
* Fishing locations
* Forgotten structures
* Difficult-to-reach secrets

Wetlands can also provide some of the island's more unusual environmental storytelling.

---

## 9.8 Deep Wilderness

The deepest wilderness should emerge naturally where geography becomes difficult.

It may combine:

* Ancient forest
* Highlands
* Cliffs
* Deep valleys
* Rivers
* Caves
* Ruins
* Strange environmental formations

This area should feel significantly removed from the town.

It represents the point where the player begins moving beyond the world they initially understand.

---

# 10. The Wild Heart

The island should contain a geographic “wild heart.”

This is not necessarily a single named zone.

It is a broader area where multiple natural systems converge.

The wild heart may include:

* Ancient forest
* Deep cave systems
* Highlands
* Hidden valleys
* Unusual waterways
* Ancient ruins

The player should gradually realize that these environments are not merely adjacent.

There is something connecting them.

The exact nature of that relationship belongs to the deeper narrative mystery.

---

# 11. Underground World

Caves and underground spaces are part of the Main Island's geography rather than merely combat dungeons.

Underground spaces may contain:

* Natural caves
* Deep caverns
* Underground rivers
* Ancient structures
* Forgotten rooms
* Mineral deposits
* Wildlife
* Hidden paths
* Strange spaces
* Historical remnants
* Impossible environments

The underground world should vary greatly.

Not every cave should lead to the same experience.

Some should simply be caves.

Others should become increasingly difficult to explain.

The relationship between the underground world and ancient forest should remain one of the major unresolved geographic questions.

---

# 12. Town Placement

The main town should sit where:

> **river + coast + countryside meet.**

This makes the settlement geographically logical.

The town benefits from:

* Farming
* Fishing
* River transportation
* Coastal trade
* Inland travel
* Fresh water
* Bridges
* Nearby farmland
* Access to wilderness

The town should therefore feel like a settlement that developed naturally over generations rather than a game hub placed at the center of a map.

---

# 13. Town as Geographic Anchor

The town is the player's primary navigational and social anchor.

From the town, players should be able to understand the broad geography of the island.

Major routes should naturally radiate outward.

Possible relationships include:

* Town → farms
* Town → meadows
* Town → woodland
* Town → coast
* Town → river
* Town → nearby settlements
* Town → wilderness
* Town → highlands

The player should gradually develop intuitive knowledge such as:

> “If I follow this river, I'll eventually reach…”

This is preferable to relying exclusively on UI markers.

---

# 14. Player Property Relationship

The inherited property should be a short journey outside town.

It should not be inside the town.

It should instead sit within a meaningful natural transition.

The journey may move through:

> Town → countryside → fields/meadows → natural landscape → home.

This gives the player a meaningful daily relationship with the surrounding world.

The property should feel connected to the community without losing its sense of personal space.

---

# 15. Natural Transitions

Biome boundaries should rarely be abrupt.

Examples:

> Town → farms → meadow → woodland

> Woodland → older woodland → ancient forest

> Highland meadow → rocky slope → cliffs

> River valley → wetlands → coastal marsh

> Woodland → rocky ground → cave entrance

Transitions are important because they make the island feel physically believable.

They also allow the player to recognize where they are without constantly checking the map.

---

# 16. Rivers

Rivers are major geographic systems.

They should influence:

* Settlement placement
* Farming
* Fishing
* Bridges
* Roads
* Exploration
* Navigation
* Wildlife
* Landscape composition
* Storytelling

Rivers should sometimes divide regions.

They should sometimes create natural routes.

They should sometimes obstruct travel.

They should sometimes become beautiful landmarks.

They should never feel like decorative blue lines placed across the map.

---

# 17. Coastline

The player should be able to explore most of the coastline.

However, some areas should remain inaccessible or difficult to reach because of:

* Cliffs
* Marshes
* Deep water
* Dangerous terrain
* Isolated coves
* Strange geography
* Natural barriers

The coastline should therefore combine familiarity and incompleteness.

The player can learn it well without ever feeling that they have reduced it to a checklist.

---

# 18. Water and Island Isolation

Water must constantly reinforce Underhallow's isolation.

The surrounding sea should feel difficult to cross because of a combination of:

* Dangerous waters
* Strange currents
* Unreliable weather
* Fog
* Navigation difficulties
* Unusual island effects
* Boats failing or disappearing
* Difficulty locating the island
* Contradictory sailor accounts

The Main Island therefore exists within a larger world without requiring that world to be immediately accessible.

---

# 19. Elevation

Elevation is a major world-design tool.

The island should primarily use:

* Rolling hills
* Valleys
* Slopes
* Raised plateaus
* Cliffs
* Depressions
* Highlands

Elevation affects:

* Visibility
* Routes
* Landmarks
* Access
* Secrets
* Water flow
* Environmental composition
* Settlement placement

Verticality should remain readable from the fixed isometric camera.

Underhallow is not intended to become a climbing simulator.

---

# 20. Ancient Structures

Ancient structures should be distributed throughout the island.

They should be **occasional but noticeable**.

Possible forms include:

* Ruined homes
* Foundations
* Roads
* Bridges
* Temples or ceremonial structures
* Agricultural structures
* Maritime structures
* Watch structures
* Underground complexes
* Unidentified constructions

Their significance should be unpredictable.

A small ruin near town may matter enormously.

A huge ruin deep in the wilderness may tell only a small part of the story.

---

# 21. Ruins and Modern Civilization

Modern civilization may literally exist on top of ancient civilization.

Examples:

* Modern houses built over old foundations
* Roads following ancient routes
* Town structures incorporating ancient stone
* Bridges replacing ancient crossings
* Farms occupying ancient agricultural land
* Buildings containing reused artifacts
* Local traditions derived from forgotten practices

This allows history to exist visibly without requiring NPCs to explain it.

---

# 22. Hidden Places

The Main Island should contain many hidden places distributed throughout the world.

Secrets should exist at different scales.

### Small secrets

* Hidden paths
* Lost items
* Tiny clearings
* Resource caches
* Forgotten objects

### Medium secrets

* Abandoned homes
* Hidden rooms
* Small ruins
* NPC-related locations
* Environmental puzzles

### Major secrets

* Impossible spaces
* Significant ruins
* Deep caves
* Historical locations
* Reality anomalies
* Places connected to the central mystery

Not every secret should connect to the main mystery.

Some should simply belong to the island.

---

# 23. Exploration Rewards

Exploration should reward the player through multiple channels.

Potential rewards include:

* Resources
* Rare items
* New activities
* NPC information
* Quests
* Shortcuts
* New locations
* Historical evidence
* Environmental discoveries
* Beautiful views
* Strange experiences
* Personal stories

Importantly:

> **Not every exploration reward needs mechanical value.**

Sometimes the reward is simply:

> “I found this beautiful strange place.”

That is a legitimate Underhallow reward.

---

# 24. Curiosity-Driven Exploration

The player should frequently be able to explore because they notice something.

Examples:

* A strange structure in the distance
* A path disappearing into trees
* A light at night
* An unusual sound
* A gap between cliffs
* An unfamiliar animal
* A partially buried structure
* Smoke from somewhere unexpected
* A river leading somewhere unknown
* An NPC mentioning a location
* A landmark visible from a hill

The world should create questions naturally.

The player should then decide whether to pursue them.

---

# 25. Early-Enterable Dangerous Areas

Some areas should technically be enterable before the player is properly prepared.

This is intentional.

A player may walk into an area and think:

> “I probably shouldn't be here yet.”

This creates genuine exploration freedom.

The game should avoid excessive invisible walls whose only purpose is to enforce progression.

Natural consequences should do more of the work.

---

# 26. World Gating

World progression should combine several types of gating.

### Geographic gating

A river, cliff, cave, marsh, or difficult terrain naturally limits access.

### Tool gating

A new tool or upgrade allows traversal or interaction.

### Knowledge gating

The player learns how to understand or navigate something.

### Social gating

An NPC relationship reveals information or grants access.

### Narrative gating

Story progression changes what is available.

### Environmental gating

Certain conditions make a location accessible or meaningful.

These should overlap rather than exist independently.

---

# 27. Regional Communities

Not every major region needs a settlement.

Some regions should contain:

* Villages
* Farms
* Fishing communities
* Remote households
* Workshops
* Outposts

Other regions should remain overwhelmingly wild.

This preserves the sense that the island contains both civilization and genuine wilderness.

---

# 28. Regional Culture

Regional communities should have strong identities.

Differences may emerge through:

* Architecture
* Clothing
* Food
* Occupations
* Traditions
* Festivals
* Local beliefs
* History
* Relationship with nature
* Relationship with the main town
* Local interpretation of the island's mysteries

Regional identity should feel organic.

The player should be able to tell where they are not only by the terrain, but by the people.

---

# 29. Island-Wide Social Network

The island should function as a connected social ecosystem.

People from distant regions may:

* Know one another
* Be related
* Trade with one another
* Visit the town
* Have old friendships
* Have rivalries
* Share history
* Disagree about historical events
* Know different pieces of the island's mysteries

The player should eventually discover that the island's communities are more interconnected than they initially appeared.

---

# 30. Navigation Philosophy

The player should be able to navigate through a combination of:

* Landmarks
* Geography
* Roads
* Rivers
* Bridges
* Buildings
* Environmental cues
* Player memory
* Map UI

The world should remain understandable without requiring constant UI dependence.

Strong landmarks should be strategically distributed.

Examples include:

* Town clock/bell tower
* Harbor
* Major bridge
* Distinctive forest
* Highland formation
* Coastal cliff
* Ancient structure
* Unusual tree
* Cave entrance
* Large rock formation

---

# 31. Landmark Design

Major landmarks should be visually identifiable from a distance where appropriate.

Landmarks serve several purposes:

1. Navigation
2. World identity
3. Memory formation
4. Environmental storytelling
5. Regional differentiation
6. Exploration motivation

A landmark should ideally answer:

> “Where am I?”

while also creating:

> “What's that?”

---

# 32. Empty Space

Not every section of the island should contain a point of interest.

Empty space is intentional.

It provides:

* Breathing room
* Peace
* Scenic composition
* Travel time
* Wildlife habitat
* Natural landscapes
* Player reflection
* Contrast against dense locations

A world filled entirely with content markers would undermine Underhallow's atmosphere.

---

# 33. Wildlife Distribution

Wildlife should follow geography.

Different regions should support different animals.

Examples:

* Farmland: domestic and grazing animals
* Meadow: small wildlife and birds
* Woodland: deer, small mammals, birds
* Ancient forest: unusual wildlife and rare species
* Highlands: mountain-adapted animals
* Coast: seabirds, marine life
* Wetlands: amphibians and water birds
* Caves: cave-adapted creatures

Most wildlife should remain grounded in recognizable nature.

Some creatures should subtly violate expectations.

---

# 34. Hunting Geography

Hunting should be connected to environmental identity.

Animals should have:

* Habitats
* Behaviors
* Preferred regions
* Time-of-day patterns
* Weather responses
* Movement patterns

Hunting should encourage the player to understand the island's ecology rather than simply search for generic enemies.

Raw hunted materials can be sold directly.

Processing is optional.

Processed materials should generally provide greater economic value, but hunters are never forced to process their kills.

---

# 35. Time and the Main Island

Day and night should meaningfully change the island.

Different times can affect:

* NPC schedules
* Wildlife
* Shops
* Activities
* Lighting
* Ambient sound
* Access
* Events
* Secrets
* Exploration atmosphere

The same location should sometimes feel like a different place at night.

This is particularly valuable for mysterious areas.

---

# 36. Weather

Weather is primarily atmospheric but may occasionally influence gameplay.

Weather can affect:

* Lighting
* Ambience
* Wildlife
* NPC behavior
* Fishing
* Farming
* Exploration
* VFX
* Special events
* Certain environmental interactions

Weather should make the island feel alive rather than functioning primarily as punishment.

---

# 37. Environmental Hazards

Environmental hazards should create meaningful decisions without becoming excessively punishing.

Potential hazards include:

* Difficult terrain
* Water
* Cliffs
* Dangerous wildlife
* Environmental conditions
* Poor visibility
* Certain hazardous areas

There is no stamina system.

The player should therefore be challenged by geography, preparation, time, equipment, and judgment rather than an energy meter.

---

# 38. Defeat and Exploration Risk

Failure on the Main Island is non-lethal.

Defeat should primarily result in:

* Lost time
* Loss of some carried resources
* Return/respawn to the Personal Island

The player should not fear exploring because failure permanently destroys progress.

The design goal is:

> **Risk creates tension without destroying curiosity.**

---

# 39. Mystery Integration

The Main Island is one of the primary delivery mechanisms for Underhallow's central mystery.

However, mystery should not dominate the player's first impression.

The progression should be:

### Layer 1 — Ordinary

The player sees:

* Farms
* Shops
* People
* Wildlife
* Fishing
* Roads
* Buildings
* Daily life

### Layer 2 — Strange

The player notices:

* Contradictory stories
* Odd traditions
* Strange ruins
* Unusual wildlife
* Places that feel wrong
* Objects that shouldn't function
* People avoiding certain subjects

### Layer 3 — Connected

The player begins realizing that apparently unrelated things are connected.

### Layer 4 — Revelation

A major truth about the island's history becomes understandable.

### Layer 5 — Uncertainty

The player realizes that understanding what happened does not necessarily mean understanding what the island ultimately is.

---

# 40. Mystery Distribution

Mystery evidence should be distributed across geography.

Different locations may contain different pieces.

Examples:

* Town contains social history.
* Farms contain inherited traditions.
* Ruins contain physical evidence.
* Ancient forest contains environmental anomalies.
* Caves contain older structures.
* Coastal areas contain maritime evidence.
* Remote communities preserve different oral histories.
* NPCs possess contradictory memories.
* The player's inherited property contains evidence connected to the grandparent.

No single location should explain everything.

---

# 41. Not Everything Connects

The island must retain independent stories.

Some secrets should:

* Have nothing to do with the central mystery.
* Belong exclusively to an NPC.
* Be remnants of ordinary historical events.
* Be unexplained but harmless.
* Simply be beautiful or strange.

This prevents the world from feeling mechanically constructed around one plot.

---

# 42. Historical Layering

The island should visibly contain multiple periods of history.

The player should encounter:

1. Modern Underhallow.
2. Older settlement layers.
3. Ancient civilization remnants.
4. Places that appear older than the known history of the island.
5. Evidence that challenges the player's understanding of what the island actually is.

These layers should coexist rather than appear only as isolated archaeological sites.

---

# 43. Main Island and the Player's Grandparent

The player's inherited property connects the personal story to the wider geography.

The grandparent was:

* A farmer
* A community member
* Quietly interested in the island's history

Their investigation should eventually point outward.

Clues may lead the player from:

> Home → Town → Local history → Ruins → Regions → Ancient sites → Deeper mystery.

The player's home therefore acts as the narrative doorway into the island.

---

# 44. Main Island and Narrative Agency

The player should not be required to investigate every mystery.

They can:

* Follow clues aggressively
* Explore casually
* Focus on farming
* Focus on hunting
* Build relationships
* Trade
* Explore selectively
* Pursue certain secrets
* Ignore others

The world should continue to function regardless.

The island should not constantly demand that the player save it.

---

# 45. Main Island and Player Identity

The player is not the island's predetermined savior.

They are a newcomer who inherits land and gradually becomes part of the community.

Their importance develops through:

* Relationships
* Contribution
* Exploration
* Choices
* Discoveries
* Actions
* Home-building

The world existed before them.

It will continue after them.

Their arrival changes parts of the island, but does not rewrite its entire history.

---

# 46. Regional Storytelling

Each major region should eventually support its own local stories.

These may involve:

* Families
* Historical conflicts
* Local traditions
* Wildlife
* Lost people
* Old buildings
* Community disputes
* Personal secrets
* Environmental changes
* Local mysteries

Some regional stories should connect to the central mystery.

Others should remain independent.

---

# 47. World Events

The Main Island may support recurring or special events such as:

* Markets
* Festivals
* Fishing events
* Community gatherings
* Seasonal celebrations
* Hunting activities
* Town projects
* Regional celebrations
* Strange environmental events
* Story-driven occurrences

Events should reinforce community rather than turn the island into a constant activity schedule.

---

# 48. Main Island Economy

The Main Island is the primary economic environment.

Activities include:

* Farming-related trade
* Fishing
* Hunting
* Gathering
* Crafting
* Resource sales
* Shops
* Services
* Trading
* NPC commerce

Economic systems should reinforce geography.

For example:

* Fishing communities should have stronger relationships with water.
* Farming communities should occupy productive land.
* Hunters should interact with wilderness.
* Traders should connect regions.
* Craftspeople should depend on local resources.

---

# 49. Main Island as a Living Place

The island must not wait for the player.

NPCs should:

* Work
* Eat
* Travel
* Socialize
* Rest
* Visit other areas
* Change routines
* React to events
* Maintain relationships

Wildlife should:

* Move through habitats
* Appear at appropriate times
* React to environmental conditions
* Occupy different regions

The environment should change naturally over time.

---

# 50. Visual Identity

The Main Island follows the established Underhallow art direction:

* Detailed modern pixel art
* SNES-era inspiration
* Fixed isometric orientation
* Natural greens
* Warm yellows
* Earthy browns
* Soft blues
* Pastel tones
* Deep nighttime purples
* Warm sunset colors
* Cozy lighting
* Strong regional identities

The world should be visually beautiful before it becomes mysterious.

---

# 51. Visual Mystery

Mystery should appear through subtle visual deviations.

Examples:

* A tree that grows strangely
* An impossible ruin alignment
* An object that appears too old
* Unusual shadows
* Strange lights
* Wildlife behaving incorrectly
* An architectural feature with no apparent purpose
* A path that seems geographically impossible
* A familiar location containing something that wasn't there before

These details should reward observation.

The player should not need a glowing quest marker to know something is interesting.

---

# 52. Environmental Storytelling

The Main Island should tell stories without dialogue.

Examples:

* Abandoned farms
* Old roads
* Broken bridges
* Reused ruins
* Strange monuments
* Ancient agricultural systems
* Old tools
* Weathered graves
* Forgotten homes
* Altered landscapes
* Objects hidden in ordinary places

The environment should frequently answer questions the player did not know to ask.

---

# 53. Exploration Density

The island should prioritize **meaningful density over raw size**.

There should be enough content that exploration feels rewarding, but enough open space that travel feels peaceful.

The target emotional rhythm is:

> Discovery → travel → quiet → discovery → social life → wilderness → home.

The island should breathe.

---

# 54. 20-Hour Experience Target

At approximately 20 hours, the player should:

* Know the town well.
* Recognize major landmarks.
* Understand the nearby countryside.
* Have explored several regions.
* Have favorite routes.
* Have discovered secrets.
* Know some NPCs personally.
* Understand basic island geography.
* Still have substantial areas and questions remaining.

The player should feel:

> “I know this place.”

followed by:

> “But I definitely don't know all of it.”

---

# 55. 100-Hour Experience Target

At approximately 100 hours, the player should feel familiar with the island.

They should recognize:

* Major routes
* Regions
* Communities
* Landmarks
* Wildlife habitats
* Important locations
* Their favorite places

However, the island should retain:

* Missed secrets
* Optional stories
* Unexplained places
* NPC depth
* Historical ambiguity
* Strange locations
* Unresolved questions

The player should feel that they know Underhallow intimately without having exhausted it conceptually.

---

# 56. Emotional View from Town Square

When the player stands in Town Square and looks toward the island, the intended emotional response is:

> **“This is peaceful. This is beautiful. I wonder what is out there.”**

Over time, that response should acquire another layer:

> **“And there is something strange about this place.”**

Eventually:

> **“I know this island. But I still don't think I understand it.”**

That progression is central to the game's identity.

---

# 57. Main Island vs. Map Completion

The Main Island should resist traditional completionist design.

The game should not communicate:

> “There are 143 locations left to discover.”

Instead, exploration should be experiential.

The player should remember:

* The strange cave they found.
* The beautiful hill overlooking the coast.
* The old house hidden in the forest.
* The NPC they met in a remote village.
* The ruin that made no sense.
* The path they discovered after years of playing.
* The place they return to simply because they like it.

The island becomes memorable through experiences, not percentages.

---

# 58. Technical World Representation

The Main Island must remain compatible with the established technical architecture.

World geography should be data-driven and authored through appropriate map tooling.

The implementation should support:

* Isometric map representation
* Terrain layers
* Collision/navigation layers
* Elevation/depth representation
* Object layers
* Interactive objects
* NPC locations
* Wildlife habitats
* Resource nodes
* Region metadata
* Landmark metadata
* Secret locations
* Story locations
* Dynamic state
* Day/night behavior
* Weather interaction

The renderer must not become the source of truth for world state.

---

# 59. Region Data Model

Each major region should conceptually define:

* Region ID
* Display name
* Geographic boundaries
* Terrain type
* Elevation characteristics
* Environmental identity
* Resource pools
* Wildlife pools
* NPC/community associations
* Landmarks
* Entrances/exits
* Traversal constraints
* Secrets
* Story content
* Time-dependent behavior
* Weather behavior
* Narrative significance
* Discovery state

Exact technical implementation belongs to the implementation architecture.

---

# 60. Performance and Scale

The Main Island should be large enough to feel substantial but controlled enough to remain performant on target PC hardware (per SR-001/ETA-001).

Design priorities:

1. Coherent geography.
2. Meaningful density.
3. Strong landmarks.
4. Reusable environmental systems.
5. Controlled visual complexity.
6. Efficient loading and rendering.
7. Avoidance of unnecessary world size.

The island should not become larger simply because larger sounds impressive.

---

# 61. Content Layering

Main Island content should be layered.

### Layer 1 — Geography

Terrain, rivers, coast, forests, hills.

### Layer 2 — Civilization

Town, roads, farms, homes, workshops, bridges.

### Layer 3 — Ecology

Wildlife, plants, fishing, resources.

### Layer 4 — Social life

NPCs, routines, communities, relationships.

### Layer 5 — Exploration

Hidden paths, caves, ruins, viewpoints, discoveries.

### Layer 6 — History

Artifacts, structures, traditions, environmental evidence.

### Layer 7 — Mystery

Anomalies, contradictions, impossible locations, deeper connections.

This layering prevents the mystery from overwhelming the everyday world.

---

# 62. Design Rules

The following rules are mandatory for Main Island design.

### Rule 1

Geography must make sense before gameplay is considered.

### Rule 2

Major regions must have meaningful identities.

### Rule 3

The world must contain genuine wilderness.

### Rule 4

Empty space is intentional.

### Rule 5

Not every secret is related to the central mystery.

### Rule 6

Not every mystery must be solved.

### Rule 7

Natural gates are preferred over arbitrary invisible barriers.

### Rule 8

Landmarks should support navigation.

### Rule 9

The player should gradually learn the island rather than instantly understand it.

### Rule 10

The world existed before the player.

### Rule 11

NPCs belong to the island independently of the player.

### Rule 12

Regions should feel connected rather than like separate levels.

### Rule 13

Water must meaningfully reinforce island identity.

### Rule 14

Elevation should influence geography without turning the game into a vertical traversal simulator.

### Rule 15

Exploration should be rewarding even when the reward is purely experiential.

### Rule 16

The Main Island should complement the Personal Island rather than compete with it.

---

# 63. Anti-Patterns

The Main Island must avoid:

* Huge empty maps created only to claim scale.
* Identical biomes with different colors.
* Artificial biome borders.
* Excessive invisible walls.
* Constant quest markers.
* Every secret being tied to the main plot.
* Every ruin containing lore exposition.
* Every strange thing being supernatural.
* Every region having identical settlements.
* NPCs existing only in town.
* Wildlife appearing randomly without habitat logic.
* Endless resource nodes everywhere.
* Excessive dungeonization of caves.
* Mandatory exploration.
* Completionist pressure.
* Geography designed solely around gameplay convenience.
* A world that feels generated around the player.
* Mystery becoming the only interesting thing about the island.

---

# 64. Final Geographic Philosophy

The Main Island should feel like a real place that happens to contain a game.

It should have:

* Geography before gameplay.
* Communities before quests.
* History before the player.
* Wilderness beyond civilization.
* Beauty before mystery.
* Mystery beneath familiarity.
* Places that matter.
* Places that simply exist.

The player should gradually move from seeing the island as a map to seeing it as a place.

---

# 65. Final Player Experience

The intended progression is:

### Arrival

> “This place is beautiful.”

### Early exploration

> “I wonder what's over there.”

### Familiarity

> “I know my way around now.”

### Curiosity

> “That's strange.”

### Discovery

> “Wait... this doesn't fit.”

### Connection

> “These things are related.”

### Revelation

> “So that's what happened.”

### Understanding

> “I finally understand this part of the island.”

### Uncertainty

> “But I still don't understand what this place really is.”

### Belonging

> “And honestly... I don't know if I want to.”

---

# 66. Canonical Main Island Statement

> **The Main Island of Underhallow is a coherent, medium-sized living world built around community, nature, exploration, history, and mystery. It should feel like a real place the player gradually learns to know and love rather than a map designed to be conquered.**

---

# 67. Canonical Main Island Principle

> **The main island of Underhallow should feel like a place you could spend years getting to know rather than a map you are expected to complete.**

This is the primary design test for all future Main Island decisions.

If a proposed feature makes the island feel more like a checklist, theme park, collection of levels, or mechanically optimized map, it should be reconsidered.

If it makes the island feel more coherent, lived-in, memorable, explorable, mysterious, or emotionally meaningful, it is likely aligned with the specification.
