# 1. World Identity

Underhallow is a cozy mystical-fantasy world built around a deceptively ordinary premise.

The player arrives on an isolated island after inheriting a piece of land from a deceased grandparent. At first, the inheritance appears simple: a home, a piece of land, and the opportunity to build a new life.

The island is beautiful.

It is peaceful.

Its people are welcoming.

Life is slow enough to breathe.

And yet something about Underhallow has never been quite right.

Ancient structures exist beneath newer settlements. Families remember histories they refuse to discuss. Travelers tell contradictory stories about the waters surrounding the island. Strange things occasionally happen in places that should be completely ordinary.

The player initially sees these things as curiosities.

Eventually, they begin to connect.

Underhallow is fundamentally a world about **building a life in a place that has a history far deeper than anyone realizes.**

The world should never lose its cozy identity simply because its mysteries become deeper.

The central creative tension is:

> **The player comes to love Underhallow before they understand it.**

---

# 2. The Island of Underhallow

## 2.1 The Name

The island has an older, forgotten name.

Nobody living in the present commonly uses it.

Most inhabitants simply call the island **Underhallow**.

The relationship between the modern name and the forgotten name is itself part of the world's history and may eventually become significant.

The old name should not be revealed casually.

It should feel like something that belongs to a much older layer of the world.

---

## 2.2 The Known World

For the foreseeable beginning of the game, **Underhallow Island is effectively the entire known world.**

The player knows that other lands may exist beyond the surrounding waters, but knowledge of them is fragmented.

Stories disagree.

Maps are incomplete.

Sailors tell different versions of what lies beyond the horizon.

Some claim distant kingdoms exist.

Others speak of islands that cannot be found twice.

Some insist the waters simply continue forever.

The important point is that the outside world exists as an idea before it exists as a reliable destination.

Underhallow is therefore not presented as one small island inside a fully mapped fantasy continent.

To the player, it is effectively the world.

This makes the island feel larger without requiring the game to immediately expose a massive external map.

---

# 3. Geographic Identity

Underhallow is a large, naturally occurring island with an irregular geography.

Its shape is defined by:

* Bays
* Peninsulas
* Rivers
* Forests
* Hills
* Farmland
* Wetlands
* Caves
* Coastlines
* Valleys
* Natural elevation
* Inland waterways
* Hidden natural formations

The island should feel geographically coherent rather than assembled as a collection of game biomes.

A forest should lead naturally into woodland.

Woodland can transition into farmland.

Farmland can transition into settlement.

Rivers should have believable relationships with terrain.

Coasts should influence settlements and travel.

Hills should create natural sightlines and routes.

The geography itself should tell the player where they are.

---

# 4. Visual Identity of the Island

Underhallow should immediately communicate:

**beautiful countryside with something old underneath it.**

Its dominant visual identity is diverse but cohesive.

The island contains enough environmental variety to support a broad sense of exploration without becoming a collection of disconnected biome showcases.

Core environmental identities include:

* Farming countryside
* Meadows
* Woodland
* Ancient forest
* Hills and highlands
* Rivers
* Lakes and ponds
* Coastline
* Beaches
* Rocky coves
* Wetlands
* Caves
* Ruined sites
* Ancient structures
* Wilderness

The player should be able to move between these environments and feel that they belong to the same island.

---

# 5. The Island's Signature

Underhallow requires a recognizable visual signature that distinguishes it from generic fantasy countryside.

Its signature should emerge from a combination of:

* An unusual coastline
* Major ancient natural landmarks
* Distinctive terrain formations
* Ancient structures integrated into the landscape
* Environmental details that subtly suggest something unusual about the island

The signature should not be a single giant fantasy monument visible from everywhere.

Underhallow should feel like a place whose identity is discovered through familiarity.

After enough time, the player should recognize its landscape almost instinctively.

---

# 6. Natural Geography

## 6.1 The Coast

The coastline contains a mixture of:

* Gentle beaches
* Rocky cliffs
* Small coves
* Sheltered bays
* Wetland edges
* Fishing areas
* Natural harbors

The coast is important to both the economy and identity of the island.

It supports:

* Fishing
* Boats
* Docks
* Trading
* Exploration
* Wildlife
* Atmospheric scenes
* Travel between islands

The sea should also be one of the first places where the player senses that Underhallow is not completely normal.

---

## 6.2 Rivers

Rivers are major geographic structures rather than decorative features.

They influence:

* Settlement
* Farming
* Fishing
* Bridges
* Travel routes
* Regional identity
* Wildlife
* Landmarks
* Exploration

The main settlement grows around an important relationship between river and harbor.

Water should help explain why the town exists where it does.

---

## 6.3 Hills and Highlands

Underhallow contains hills and elevated terrain rather than enormous mountains dominating the world.

Elevation affects:

* Visibility
* Exploration
* Access
* Route selection
* Environmental composition
* Landmarks
* Hidden locations

Higher areas should occasionally allow the player to look back over places they have previously visited.

This reinforces the feeling that the island is one connected place.

---

# 7. The Wild Heart of Underhallow

Deep within the island lies a transition from ordinary countryside into increasingly old and unusual wilderness.

The deepest wilderness is primarily represented by:

* Ancient forest
* Deep cave systems
* Isolated natural spaces
* Ruined areas
* Strange environmental formations

These locations should initially feel like difficult but beautiful wilderness.

Over time, the player discovers that some of these places are connected to the island's deeper history.

The wilderness should therefore function on two levels:

**Gameplay level:**
A place to explore, gather resources, hunt, discover secrets, and encounter wildlife.

**Narrative level:**
A place where the modern world becomes increasingly thin and the ancient world begins to show through.

---

# 8. The Town

The central settlement is an ancient but prosperous small town.

It is not a struggling medieval village.

It is not a huge city.

It is a comfortable, established community that has survived for generations.

The town feels:

* Prosperous
* Sleepy
* Welcoming
* Old
* Lived-in
* Charming
* Almost suspiciously normal

The town functions as the social and practical heart of the island.

---

# 9. Town Geography

The town developed around the meeting of an important river system and harbor.

This creates a natural relationship between:

* Town Square
* River
* Harbor
* Market
* Residential areas
* Workshops
* Farms
* Roads
* Bridges

The Town Square is the social center.

The harbor provides the town with its connection to the wider island and the possibility of travel beyond it.

The surrounding countryside gradually transitions outward:

**Town → farms/meadows → woodland → deeper wilderness**

The player should never feel as though they have crossed an arbitrary biome boundary.

---

# 10. Town Architecture

The town contains multiple architectural generations.

Buildings may include:

* Timber houses
* Stone buildings
* Plaster homes
* Inns
* Workshops
* Barns
* Market stalls
* Warehouses
* Farm structures
* Bridges
* Towers
* Older foundations
* Ancient structures

The town is predominantly old-but-maintained.

Some buildings are relatively new.

Some have existed for generations.

Some contain foundations or architectural elements considerably older than the buildings currently standing above them.

This creates an important visual metaphor:

> **New life has been built on top of old history.**

---

# 11. Town Landmark

The town's visual identity is anchored by a combination of major landmarks.

The most important include:

* A historic stone bridge
* A bell or clock tower
* A significant harbor structure

These landmarks should help the player navigate without relying entirely on a map.

They should also become familiar emotional anchors.

A player should eventually be able to think:

> “I'm near the old bridge.”

and immediately understand where they are.

---

# 12. The People of Underhallow

Underhallow is a close-knit community.

Everyone knows everyone.

People know each other's families.

They know each other's businesses.

They know old arguments.

They know old relationships.

They know who grew up where.

And when the player arrives, **everyone knows who they are before they know anyone else.**

This creates an unusual newcomer dynamic.

The player is technically an outsider.

But they are not treated as an enemy or stranger.

They are welcomed.

People are generally kind.

They offer advice.

They invite the player into community life.

They may even know details about the player's inherited property before the player does.

The player is therefore not entering a hostile mystery.

They are entering a community that already has a history with the land they inherited.

---

# 13. The Town's Relationship With Its Past

Most residents do not openly discuss the island's deeper history.

This does not mean everyone is secretly evil.

Different people have different relationships with the past.

Some:

* Know fragments
* Believe old stories
* Remember family warnings
* Have experienced strange events
* Protect traditions
* Avoid certain places
* Believe the official history
* Reject the old stories entirely

Some families deliberately hid information across generations.

A small number of people actively protect certain secrets.

No single person should possess the entire truth.

The deeper history is fragmented across people, families, communities, locations, objects, and traditions.

---

# 14. Ancient Civilization

Long before the current inhabitants established their modern communities, another civilization existed on the island.

Its exact identity remains deliberately incomplete.

It was sophisticated in several areas, including:

* Agriculture
* Maritime activity
* Environmental knowledge
* Understanding of the island

Its greatest significance is not necessarily what it built.

It is what it **knew**.

The ancient civilization appears to have understood something fundamental about Underhallow that modern inhabitants no longer fully understand.

---

# 15. The Disappearance

The ancient civilization did not simply leave behind a clean historical endpoint.

Something happened.

The modern understanding of that event is incomplete.

Some records imply deliberate concealment.

Some ruins suggest abandonment.

Some traditions imply disaster.

Some evidence contradicts all of these interpretations.

The disappearance should therefore remain one of the historical layers leading toward the central mystery.

The player should gradually realize:

> The accepted history of Underhallow is not necessarily false.

> It is simply incomplete.

---

# 16. Ancient Remnants

Evidence of the ancient civilization remains in multiple forms:

* Ruins
* Artifacts
* Architectural foundations
* Language fragments
* Traditions
* Symbols
* Stories
* Objects
* Unusual technologies or mechanisms
* Places that still function

Ancient structures should not be everywhere.

They are special.

Finding one should feel meaningful.

Some ruins are beautiful.

Some are familiar enough to understand.

Some are strangely uncomfortable.

Some may appear to have been designed for purposes the player cannot immediately understand.

---

# 17. Layers of History

Underhallow's history should feel layered.

A modern house may stand on an older foundation.

A road may follow an ancient route.

A town tradition may originate from an event nobody remembers.

A harmless festival may contain remnants of an ancient ritual.

A family saying may preserve a warning whose original meaning has been forgotten.

A field may have been cultivated for centuries.

The world should repeatedly communicate:

> **People have been living here for much longer than anyone realizes.**

---

# 18. The Central Mystery

The central mystery is not one isolated question.

It is a collection of apparently unrelated mysteries that gradually reveal themselves as parts of the same underlying truth.

The player encounters:

* Strange places
* Contradictory histories
* Ancient ruins
* Unusual wildlife
* Disappearing or unreliable travel
* Family secrets
* Strange traditions
* Unexplained objects
* Reality-breaking locations
* Conflicting stories about the island

At first these appear unrelated.

Eventually they begin connecting.

The player should experience a gradual transition:

> “This place is a little strange.”

into:

> **“Wait... what the hell is this place?”**

---

# 19. The Mystery Curve

The mystery should develop gradually.

The player should not begin the game believing they are investigating a supernatural conspiracy.

The initial experience is ordinary life.

The mystery enters through small inconsistencies.

Examples include:

* An NPC refusing to explain an old tradition
* A location locals avoid without obvious reason
* An ancient object found somewhere it should not be
* An animal behaving strangely
* A family knowing something about the player's property
* A map that does not completely match reality
* A story that changes depending on who tells it

These clues accumulate.

Eventually one major revelation changes the player's understanding of the island.

From that point onward, earlier details gain new meaning.

---

# 20. The Supernatural

Underhallow should never provide a completely comfortable answer to the question:

> **Is the island itself supernatural?**

The player should find compelling evidence that something is fundamentally unusual.

But complete certainty should remain impossible.

The island may be:

* Supernatural
* Connected to something supernatural
* A place where unusual natural laws operate
* Influenced by something beyond ordinary understanding
* Or something the player cannot properly categorize

The game should allow the player to understand what is happening without necessarily allowing them to fully explain **why**.

---

# 21. Reality-Breaking Places

Certain locations behave differently from ordinary places.

These locations may contain:

* Altered space
* Strange time behavior
* Impossible geography
* Fragments of the past
* Unusual environmental phenomena
* Beautiful but unsettling spaces
* Places where ordinary rules appear unreliable

Different locations should behave differently.

There should not be a single standardized “magic zone.”

Some anomalies may be frightening.

Others may be peaceful.

Some may be breathtakingly beautiful.

The most effective anomalies are often those that appear peaceful while making the player deeply uncomfortable.

---

# 22. Strange Wildlife

Most wildlife in Underhallow is recognizable.

Animals behave like animals.

The island supports familiar species associated with forests, farmland, wetlands, and coastal environments.

However, occasional creatures do not quite fit.

Some may have:

* Unusual anatomy
* Strange proportions
* Unnatural movement
* Unusual eyes
* Strange coloration
* Peculiar behavior
* Environmental effects around them

These creatures should not immediately look like evil monsters.

They should look **wrong in a subtle way**.

The player may initially wonder whether they simply saw something unusual.

Only later should some encounters gain greater significance.

---

# 23. Regional Communities

Different regions of the island should have their own communities, customs, livelihoods, and personalities.

These communities should not simply be alternate versions of the central town.

Regional identity can emerge through:

* Local industries
* Geography
* Family histories
* Architecture
* Food
* Festivals
* Dialects or expressions
* Beliefs
* Relationships with nature
* Attitudes toward the island's past

Each community should contain its own small stories.

Some will connect to the larger mystery.

Others may simply exist because people have ordinary lives.

Not every story needs to be secretly connected to the central plot.

---

# 24. Regional Mysteries

Each major region should contain secrets.

These can include:

* Hidden locations
* Lost objects
* Family stories
* Old ruins
* Environmental anomalies
* Forgotten events
* Local legends
* Strange creatures
* Abandoned structures

Some regional mysteries connect directly to the central mystery.

Some provide partial evidence.

Some remain independent.

This distinction is important.

The world should not feel like every rock exists solely to explain the main plot.

---

# 25. Hidden Locations

Underhallow rewards curiosity.

Hidden places can include:

* Caves
* Concealed paths
* Hidden rooms
* Secret passages
* Isolated clearings
* Unusual environmental spaces
* Ruined structures
* Lore locations
* Places accessible only under certain conditions

Discovery should often be optional.

The player should never feel forced to uncover everything.

Some secrets exist simply because the world is worth exploring.

---

# 26. The Outside World

The outside world exists beyond Underhallow, but its true nature is uncertain.

Different residents believe different things.

Some believe there are normal kingdoms.

Some believe there are distant islands.

Some believe there are enormous civilizations.

Some believe almost nothing lies beyond the surrounding waters.

Some stories contradict one another so dramatically that the player cannot determine which, if any, are true.

Eventually, the outside world may become significantly important.

But it should never immediately destroy Underhallow's identity as the player's world.

---

# 27. The Isolation of Underhallow

Leaving the island is difficult.

Possible explanations include:

* Strange currents
* Dense fog
* Unreliable navigation
* Dangerous waters
* Boats failing
* Ships disappearing
* People failing to return
* Routes changing
* Contradictory sailor accounts

No single explanation should immediately be confirmed as the truth.

The player should gradually realize that Underhallow's isolation is more complicated than ordinary geography.

---

# 28. The Island and the Outside World

One of the deeper questions of the game is whether Underhallow is isolated **from** the outside world or isolated **because of** the outside world.

This distinction should remain important.

The island may be:

* Hidden
* Protected
* Contained
* Forgotten
* Avoided
* Isolated by natural forces
* Isolating itself
* Or connected to something much stranger

The game should allow evidence for multiple interpretations before eventually providing a deeper understanding.

---

# 29. The Player's Inheritance

The player's story begins with an inheritance.

A deceased grandparent has left the player a property on Underhallow.

Initially, this appears completely ordinary.

The player believes they inherited:

* A house
* Land
* A chance at a new life

That is all.

The inheritance should not immediately announce itself as a plot device.

Its deeper significance emerges gradually.

Eventually, the player discovers that the reason they received the property may be directly connected to the island's deeper history.

---

# 30. The Grandparent

The grandparent was a respected but not necessarily famous member of the community.

They were connected to ordinary island life.

They may have been a farmer or community member while also quietly paying attention to the island's unusual history.

Their public identity should be understandable.

Their private history should be considerably more complicated.

The player initially remembers or understands very little about them.

This creates a personal mystery alongside the world's mystery.

---

# 31. The Inherited Property

The property begins as the player's home.

It is not initially presented as an ancient mystery site.

Its importance should emerge naturally.

The property may contain:

* Old belongings
* Strange objects
* Unusual documents
* Hidden spaces
* Altered structures
* Evidence of previous activity
* Signs that the grandparent knew more than they revealed

The player should first think:

> “I inherited a farm.”

Only later:

> “Why did they leave this to me?”

And eventually:

> **“Why was I specifically supposed to have this place?”**

---

# 32. Home

The player's home is one of the most important symbols in the world.

It represents:

* Progression
* Stability
* Identity
* Belonging
* Personal expression
* Memory
* The life the player has built

The house should grow alongside the player.

It begins modestly.

It becomes a substantial homestead.

Eventually it can become an impressive estate.

Customization remains important throughout the progression.

The home is therefore not merely an upgrade ladder.

It is the physical record of the player's life on Underhallow.

---

# 33. The Personal Island

The player's personal island provides a private space separate from the main island.

It contains:

* The player's home
* Farming areas
* Building space
* Personal expression
* Unlockable grids
* Expandable possibilities

The personal island should feel safe and familiar.

It contrasts with the mystery of the main island.

As the player's relationship with Underhallow develops, the personal island becomes increasingly meaningful.

It is the place the player returns to after exploration.

It is home.

---

# 34. Guild Islands

Guild Islands are part of the future world architecture.

They are communal spaces that may eventually support:

* Shared construction
* Social activity
* Community projects
* Guild identity
* Multiplayer interaction

They should remain compatible with the single-player-first identity of the initial game.

The existence of guild spaces should not compromise the feeling that Underhallow can be experienced as a personal world.

---

# 35. Day and Night

Underhallow follows a traditional day/night cycle.

Time affects the world naturally.

It influences:

* NPC schedules
* Creature behavior
* Shops and services
* Available interactions
* Lighting
* Atmosphere
* Events
* Exploration opportunities
* Certain secrets

Night should not simply be “the same map but darker.”

It should reveal another personality of the island.

The cozy world becomes quieter.

The strange world becomes more noticeable.

---

# 36. Weather

Weather is primarily atmospheric.

It can influence:

* Lighting
* Ambience
* VFX
* NPC behavior
* Wildlife
* Farming
* Exploration
* Special events

Weather should generally enhance the feeling of living in a real place rather than functioning primarily as punishment.

Rain should feel like rain.

Fog should feel beautiful before it becomes unsettling.

Storms should make the island feel alive.

---

# 37. Environmental Hazards

Environmental hazards exist but should not be designed around harsh punishment.

Underhallow does not use a stamina system.

Hazards should instead create:

* Navigation challenges
* Environmental storytelling
* Route decisions
* Exploration tension
* Occasional resource loss
* Situational danger

The world can be dangerous without becoming exhausting.

---

# 38. Defeat and Consequences

Defeat is non-lethal.

The player does not permanently die.

Instead, defeat can result in:

* Loss of time
* Loss of some carried resources
* Respawning on the personal island

The world should maintain a sense of consequence without becoming oppressive.

The player should feel encouraged to explore.

---

# 39. The Economy

The island's economy is rooted in ordinary community life.

Major economic activities include:

* Farming
* Fishing
* Hunting
* Trading
* Local services
* Crafting
* Resource gathering

The economy should feel like something that naturally developed from the geography of the island.

Coastal communities fish.

Farm communities cultivate land.

Hunters gather resources from wilderness.

Traders connect communities.

Craftspeople transform raw materials.

---

# 40. Farming

Farming is one of the primary expressions of the player's life.

It is not merely a resource-generation mechanic.

Farming contributes to:

* Home
* Routine
* Community
* Economy
* Progression
* Seasonal atmosphere
* Personal identity

The player's farm should visually demonstrate how their life has changed.

A neglected patch of land can gradually become something the player is proud of.

---

# 41. Hunting

Hunting is an important relationship between the player and the island's wilderness.

Hunters gather raw resources from wildlife.

Processing those resources can increase their value.

However:

> **Hunters are never forced to process their kills.**

A hunter can sell raw materials for a lower value if they prefer.

Processing is therefore an economic optimization rather than a mandatory step.

This distinction preserves role freedom.

---

# 42. Exploration

Exploration is driven by curiosity rather than checklist completion.

The player should constantly encounter reasons to wonder:

* What is beyond that hill?
* Why does this path end here?
* Who built this?
* Why do locals avoid this place?
* Why does that animal behave differently?
* Why is this symbol everywhere?
* Why does this map not make sense?

Exploration should reward observation.

The world should frequently provide something interesting without demanding that the player investigate it immediately.

---

# 43. Mystery and Ordinary Life

The central design principle of Underhallow is that mystery should coexist with ordinary life.

The player can spend a day:

* Planting crops
* Fishing
* Hunting
* Building
* Talking to an NPC
* Buying supplies
* Exploring
* Returning home

And then encounter something inexplicable.

The player can ignore it.

They can continue farming.

They can return to it later.

This freedom is essential.

The mystery should invite curiosity rather than dictate the player's schedule.

---

# 44. Moral Complexity

The deeper mystery should not reduce to:

**Good people vs evil people.**

There should be no completely innocent or completely guilty side.

Different people should have understandable reasons for what they do.

Some conceal information because they are afraid.

Some protect others.

Some protect themselves.

Some genuinely believe the truth is dangerous.

Some may believe the island's secrets should be exposed.

Others may believe certain things should remain buried.

The player should eventually be forced to consider:

> **Is discovering the truth always the right thing to do?**

---

# 45. Truth and Knowledge

Underhallow does not treat knowledge as automatically equivalent to goodness.

The game should explore the distinction between:

* Knowing
* Understanding
* Controlling
* Changing
* Accepting

The player may eventually understand something without being capable of fully explaining it.

They may discover a truth without having the right to control its consequences.

They may learn something that changes how they view the island without making the island any less beautiful.

---

# 46. Thematic Foundation

The world is built around several central themes.

### Nature is beautiful.

The island should remain beautiful even when it becomes strange.

### People carry histories.

Every person and community exists within layers of memory.

### The world is bigger than you.

The player can become important without becoming the center of existence.

### Home is something you build.

The player's home is created through accumulated choices and effort.

### Some mysteries can be left alone.

Not every unknown needs to be conquered.

### Understanding does not mean control.

The player can understand the island without owning its deepest secrets.

---

# 47. The Emotional Arc

The player's emotional relationship with Underhallow should evolve approximately as follows:

### Stage 1 — Arrival

“This is a beautiful place.”

### Stage 2 — Belonging

“I could actually live here.”

### Stage 3 — Curiosity

“There are some strange things going on.”

### Stage 4 — Suspicion

“Why does everyone seem to know something I don't?”

### Stage 5 — Revelation

“This island is much older and stranger than I thought.”

### Stage 6 — Understanding

“I finally understand what some of this means.”

### Stage 7 — Uncertainty

“But I still don't understand everything.”

### Stage 8 — Acceptance

“And maybe I don't need to.”

The player's love for the world should survive every stage.

---

# 48. The World at 100 Hours

After a long time in Underhallow, the player should be able to look across their home and recognize their history.

They have:

* Built their house
* Developed their land
* Met the community
* Explored the island
* Discovered secrets
* Formed relationships
* Seen strange places
* Learned fragments of history
* Changed parts of the world
* Made choices about what to pursue

Yet the island should still contain things they cannot completely explain.

The intended emotional response is:

> **“This is my little corner of the world.”**

followed by:

> **“I still don't understand everything.”**

and finally:

> **“And that's okay.”**

---

# 49. World-Building Rule

Underhallow should always maintain the following balance:

**Cozy surface.
Mystical middle.
Deep mystery underneath.**

The player should be able to enjoy Underhallow without caring about its deepest secrets.

But players who become curious should find increasingly meaningful layers beneath the surface.

The game should reward curiosity without punishing indifference.

---

# 50. The Core World Statement

> **Underhallow is a peaceful, welcoming island where people farm, fish, hunt, trade, build homes, raise families, and live ordinary lives. Beneath that ordinary life lies a history older than anyone remembers, fragmented across ruins, families, traditions, strange places, and secrets. The player arrives as an outsider and slowly discovers that the land they inherited is connected to something much deeper than they could have imagined.**

The mystery should never erase the life.

The life should never erase the mystery.

---

# 51. Canonical World Philosophy

Underhallow should ultimately feel like:

> **A mysterious place where you build your life even though you don't fully understand it — and eventually come to love it enough that you aren't sure you want every mystery solved.**

Or, more simply:

> **Under a lazy place where you build your life, there is something much deeper.**

The player's greatest achievement is not conquering the island.

It is making a home within it.

And perhaps the most important discovery is not what Underhallow is.

It is realizing that **home can still be home even when you don't understand everything beneath it.**
